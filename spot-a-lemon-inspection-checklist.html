How to Spot a Lemon: A Pre-Purchase Inspection Checklist

Inspections · 11 min read

When you're financing a car over four or five years, the vehicle's condition stops being a comfort question and becomes a credit question. A transmission failure in month nine doesn't just cost you the repair — it puts you in a month where you have to choose between the repair and the payment. And one payment 30 days late sits on your credit file for about six years.

Which makes this the highest-stakes twenty minutes of the entire process. Here's what to actually look at, in order, and how to know when to stop looking and start walking.

📌 Key takeaways
  • An independent inspection is $100 to $250 and is the best money you'll spend. Your own walkaround decides which cars are worth paying for one.
  • Inspect in daylight and dry weather. Rain and dusk hide paint defects, panel work and leaks.
  • Insist on a cold start. A pre-warmed engine hides smoke, noise and hard starting.
  • An Ontario safety certificate is a licensing document, not a quality assessment. Don't mistake one for the other.

Set the conditions first

Before you look at anything, control the circumstances. Sellers who know what they're doing control them for you, and not in your favour.

  • Daylight, and dry. Wet paint hides swirl marks, mismatched panels, orange-peel texture from a respray, and dents. Dusk and streetlights hide all of the same. A seller who is only available after dark is worth being curious about.
  • The engine must be cold. Say this when you arrange the viewing: "please don't start it before I arrive." A warm engine conceals hard starting, startup rattle, blue or white smoke, and rough idle. If you arrive and the hood is warm, ask why and consider coming back.
  • Clean, level ground where you can see whether anything is dripping, and where you can sight down the panels.
  • Bring a few things: a flashlight, a fridge magnet, a rag, gloves, an OBD-II scanner if you have one, and a tire tread depth gauge or a coin.
  • Bring a second person if you can. Someone to stand outside while you operate the lights and brakes, and someone to slow you down if you fall in love with the car.

Body, paint and panel gaps

You're looking for evidence of collision repair — including the kind that never appeared on the vehicle history report because it was paid for in cash.

Sight down each side of the car from the front corner and the rear corner, at a low angle. Reflections should run smoothly along the body line. Waves, ripples or dull patches mean filler or a repaint.

Compare panel gaps left to right. The gap around the hood, the doors, the trunk and the fenders should be even and symmetrical. A gap that's tight at the top and wide at the bottom, or noticeably different from the same gap on the other side, means panels have been off or the structure has moved.

Check for overspray. Look at the rubber weatherstripping around the doors and windows, the plastic trim, the wheel wells and the inside of the door jambs. Paint mist where paint shouldn't be, or a faint tape line, means a respray. Look inside the fuel filler door too — factory paint there should match the exterior exactly.

Look at bolt heads. Open the hood and the doors and examine the bolts holding the fenders and the hinges. Factory bolts are usually uniform and undisturbed; if the paint on a bolt head is chipped, if there are wrench marks, or if a bolt looks newer than its neighbours, that panel has been removed.

Use the magnet. A fridge magnet should stick firmly to steel body panels. Where it holds weakly or slides, there may be body filler underneath. Note the limitation: many modern vehicles use aluminum or composite hoods, fenders and trunk lids, where a magnet won't stick at all and that's completely normal. Check what the panel is made of before drawing a conclusion.

Check the glass. Each piece of glass usually carries a small manufacturer marking with a date code. If three windows match and one is from a different maker or a much later date, that window was replaced — possibly routine, possibly a clue about an impact.

Rust: the Canadian section

This is where a car bought in Ontario or Quebec differs most from the same car bought in a dry province, and where the expensive surprises live. Get on the ground with a flashlight. It's worth the dirty knees.

Learn the three stages. Surface rust is cosmetic discolouration you can often wipe or sand — common and not alarming on an older Canadian car. Scale is flaking, layered rust that's eating into the metal, and it will keep going. Perforation is a hole. Perforation on a structural member is a different category of problem from perforation on a rocker panel's outer skin.

Where to look, in priority order:

  • Brake lines and fuel lines. Follow them along the underside. Corroded, scaly, or flaking lines are both a safety-inspection failure and expensive to replace properly. This is the single most commonly overlooked item on a salt-belt car.
  • Rocker panels and the bottoms of the doors, including inside the lower door edge where water collects.
  • Wheel arches, front and rear, and the inner lip you can only see by turning the wheels.
  • Subframes, suspension mounting points and strut towers. Open the hood and look at the towers; open the trunk and look at the rear mounting areas. Rust here is structural.
  • Trunk floor and the spare tire well. Lift the mat and the spare. Standing water, rust stains, or a musty smell means a leak, and a spare well full of rust means water has been sitting there for years.
  • Exhaust and its hangers, and the fuel tank straps.
  • Under the carpet in the footwells if you can lift an edge, especially on the driver's side.

A car with an unusually clean underside for its age and province is worth a second thought too. Fresh undercoating sprayed over existing rust is a known way to hide it, and it makes proper assessment harder. Ask when it was applied and by whom.

Tires tell you about the suspension

Tires are the cheapest diagnostic on the car, and they report on components that cost far more than tires.

Are all four the same? Same brand, same model, same size, similar wear. A mismatched set means the car has been maintained on the cheap, and on an all-wheel-drive vehicle mismatched circumferences can damage the drivetrain.

Read the date codes. Every tire has a four-digit DOT code stamped in the sidewall giving the week and year of manufacture — 2224 means the 22nd week of 2024. Rubber ages regardless of tread, and tires more than about six years old should be replaced whatever they look like. Four aged tires is a real cost to factor into the price.

Read the wear pattern, and run your hand over the tread in both directions:

  • Worn on both outer edges, centre fine — chronic underinflation.
  • Worn in the centre only — chronic overinflation.
  • Worn on one edge only — alignment or worn suspension components. A camber issue can also point to accident damage.
  • Cupping or scalloping — a wavy pattern you feel as ridges — usually means worn shocks or struts.
  • Feathering, where tread blocks are sharp on one side and rounded on the other — toe misalignment.

Also check tread depth on all four with a gauge, and look for sidewall bulges, cracking between tread blocks, and any repair patches. Then look at the brake rotors through the wheels: a deep lip at the outer edge, heavy scoring, or a rusty ridge means rotors are near the end.

Fluids and the engine bay

Do this with the engine still cold and off.

Engine oil. Pull the dipstick and wipe it on a rag. You want an amber-to-dark-brown film. Milky, tan, or the colour of a latte means coolant is getting into the oil — head gasket or worse, and a reason to stop right there. Gritty texture between your fingers means metal. Oil filled well above the maximum mark can indicate fuel dilution.

Coolant. Look in the reservoir, not the radiator cap, and only when cold. It should be the correct colour for the vehicle and reasonably clear. Rusty brown means the system hasn't been serviced. An oily film or froth on the surface is the same head gasket warning from the other direction.

Transmission fluid, if the vehicle has a dipstick — many newer ones don't. It should be reddish and shouldn't smell burnt. A dark brown fluid with a scorched smell is a significant finding on an automatic, and transmission work is among the most expensive repairs on a used car.

Brake fluid. Very dark fluid means it's old. Low fluid with no obvious explanation deserves attention.

Then look around the bay. Crusty white or coloured residue trails show where something has been weeping. Look at the ground under the car and at the underside of the engine. Check belts for cracking and glazing, and squeeze the coolant hoses — they should be firm, not spongy or rock-hard.

Be suspicious of a spotless engine bay in an older car. Steam cleaning an engine right before a sale is a standard way to erase the evidence of active leaks. It's not proof of anything, but it means you can't use the leak evidence, which is exactly the point. Combine it with a fresh look underneath after the test drive, when things are hot and any leak is most likely to show.

The cold start

Twenty seconds that tell you a lot. Stand outside the car at the back while someone else turns the key, so you can see and hear the exhaust.

  • How does it crank? It should catch quickly. Extended cranking, or catching and nearly stalling, is worth investigating.
  • Blue smoke from the exhaust on startup means oil is being burned — worn valve guides, seals or rings.
  • White smoke that persists after the engine warms is coolant burning, pointing to a head gasket. A brief white vapour cloud on a cold Canadian morning that clears quickly is just condensation and is normal.
  • Black smoke means it's running rich.
  • A rattle for the first second or two can indicate a worn timing chain tensioner or low oil pressure on startup. On some engines this is a known and expensive issue.
  • Listen at idle for ticking, knocking, or an irregular beat. Then rev it gently and listen again.
  • Watch the dashboard through the startup sequence. All the warning lights should illuminate briefly and then go out. A light that never comes on at all — particularly the check engine light or the airbag light — can mean the bulb was removed or the cluster was tampered with to hide a fault. This is a common trick and easy to catch if you know to watch for it.

Interior, electrics and water

Smell first, before you look at anything. Musty or mildewed means water is getting in, or has. Heavy air freshener is worth noticing — sometimes it's covering mildew, smoke, or a coolant smell. Press the carpet in the footwells and the trunk with your palm and check whether it's damp underneath.

Water damage deserves particular caution on a financed car. Flood-related electrical faults surface unpredictably for years, and there is no efficient way to chase them. A car with a water history is a poor choice for a multi-year loan.

Check wear against the odometer. The driver's seat bolster, the steering wheel rim, the gear selector, the pedal rubbers and the driver's door armrest all wear at a predictable rate. A car showing 70,000 kilometres with a shiny worn steering wheel and a polished brake pedal is telling you the number is wrong, or that it lived a much harder life than the number suggests.

Then test every single electrical item. This is tedious and it's where money hides:

  • Every window, from every switch, including the driver's master switch.
  • Every door lock, the key fob, and the trunk release.
  • Heat on full, then air conditioning on full — people skip A/C in winter and discover a $1,200 problem in June.
  • Rear defroster, heated seats, heated steering wheel, heated mirrors.
  • All exterior lights: headlights on both beams, signals, brake lights, reverse lights, fog lights, hazards. This is where the second person earns their coffee.
  • Wipers on all speeds, washer fluid spray, rear wiper.
  • The infotainment screen, backup camera, speakers, Bluetooth pairing, and every USB port.
  • Sunroof, fully open and fully closed, and check its drain channels for debris.
  • The horn, the parking brake, and the seatbelts — each one should retract smoothly and latch firmly.

The test drive, properly done

Ten minutes around the block is not a test drive. Aim for twenty to thirty minutes with both city and highway driving, and turn the radio off for all of it.

  • At very low speed, windows down, turn the wheel to full lock in each direction and drive slowly. Clicking or popping points to worn CV joints. Groaning suggests power steering.
  • Over rough pavement, listen for clunks and rattles from the suspension. A single clunk over a bump is often a worn sway bar link or bushing; a heavy thud can be a strut mount.
  • On a flat, straight, empty stretch, briefly relax your grip. The car shouldn't pull to either side. Pulling means alignment, tires, or brakes — and can indicate past structural damage.
  • Brake hard from about 60 km/h in a safe, clear place. Pulsing through the pedal means warped rotors. Pulling to one side means a sticking caliper. A soft or sinking pedal is a hydraulic problem and a reason to end the drive.
  • Accelerate hard from a stop, then let it upshift gently. An automatic should shift smoothly without flaring, slipping or jolting. Hesitation, a rise in engine speed without acceleration, or a hard bang between gears are all serious findings.
  • At highway speed, check for vibration through the wheel or the seat, and listen for a droning hum that rises and falls with speed — a wheel bearing. Then change lanes gently a few times and see if the note changes.
  • Reverse, and reverse while turning. Some faults only appear here.
  • Test cruise control and, if equipped, any driver-assist features.
  • At the end, let it idle for five minutes, then get out and look underneath for drips and check whether the temperature gauge is holding steady. Also switch the A/C on at hot idle and confirm the cooling fans run.

Then shut it off, wait a minute, and start it again hot. Hot restart problems are a separate category from cold start problems.

The $30 tool that catches cleared codes

An OBD-II reader plugs into a port under the dash on any vehicle from the mid-1990s onward. Basic ones cost about $30, or you can use a phone app with a Bluetooth dongle. Two things to check, and the second is the one most people miss.

Stored and pending codes. Stored codes are active faults. Pending codes are faults the computer has seen once and is waiting to confirm — meaning a problem that hasn't yet turned the light on. Pending codes on a car with no illuminated warning light are genuinely useful information.

Readiness monitor status. This is the clever part. The computer runs a series of self-tests — catalyst, oxygen sensor, EVAP system and others — and each is flagged as complete or incomplete. Clearing codes resets all of them to incomplete, and they take a mix of driving conditions to finish. So if you plug in and find several monitors "not ready" on a car that's been driven regularly, somebody likely cleared the codes recently — very often right before showing it to you, to make a check engine light disappear for a few days. The light will come back.

Ask about it directly. A seller with an innocent explanation, like a recently replaced battery, will say so.

"Plugged in the reader and four monitors weren't ready. Seller said the battery had been changed. It hadn't — the light came on for the next buyer two days later. That thirty-dollar gadget saved me from a catalytic converter."

Paperwork and VIN matching

Quick, and non-negotiable.

  • Match the VIN in at least two places on the car — the plate at the base of the windshield and the sticker in the driver's door jamb — against the ownership documents and the history report. All characters, not the last four.
  • Look at the door jamb sticker itself. If it appears reprinted, peeling oddly, or freshly applied, stop.
  • Confirm the seller's name matches the registration. If it doesn't, get an explanation you can verify before any money moves.
  • Ask for service records. A folder of receipts is one of the best signals available. Note whether the maintenance was done at reasonable intervals and whether major services — timing belt, transmission fluid — have been performed.
  • Run your own lien search if you're buying privately — PPSA in Ontario, RDPRM in Quebec — close to the day you pay. Why this matters so much in a private sale.
  • Check open recalls by VIN through Transport Canada or the manufacturer. Recall work is usually free, so this is pure upside.

And to be explicit about a common misunderstanding: an Ontario Safety Standards Certificate is not a quality inspection. It confirms minimum safety requirements were met on the day of inspection. It says nothing about the engine, the transmission, the air conditioning, or accident history, and it isn't a warranty. A certificate is not a substitute for anything in this article.

The professional inspection

Everything above is triage. Its purpose is to decide which cars deserve the real inspection, because you can't afford to pay for one on every car you look at — and you absolutely should pay for one on the car you're about to buy.

Take it to an independent shop, not the selling dealer's service department, and not a shop the seller recommends. Mobile inspectors who come to the vehicle are a reasonable alternative when a lift isn't possible, though a lift is better.

Ask for these specifically:

  • The vehicle on a hoist, with the underside, subframes, suspension, exhaust, and brake and fuel lines examined.
  • A full scan, including stored codes, pending codes and readiness monitors.
  • Measured brake pad and rotor thickness and tire tread depth, in numbers rather than "fine."
  • All fluid conditions assessed, and any leaks identified by source.
  • An assessment of collision repair — evidence of structural work, panel replacement, and repair quality.
  • A road test by the technician.
  • A compression or leak-down test if anything about the engine's behaviour warranted it.
  • A written report with photographs, separating what needs doing now from what will need doing within a year, with rough costs.

That last item is what turns an inspection into negotiating leverage. "It needs $1,400 of work in the next six months" is a specific, documented argument about price.

If the seller won't allow an independent inspection, that is your answer. Not a reason to push harder — a reason to leave. A seller with a good car has nothing to lose from a mechanic looking at it, and plenty to gain.

None of this requires being a mechanic. It requires being unhurried and slightly skeptical for half an hour, and being willing to spend a couple of hundred dollars to check a purchase you'll be paying for over years. The cars that fail this routine will show it quickly. The one that passes is the one worth financing.

Frequently asked questions

What should a pre-purchase inspection include?

At minimum: the vehicle on a lift so the underside, suspension, exhaust, and brake and fuel lines can be examined properly; a scan of the onboard computer for stored and pending fault codes plus readiness monitor status; measurement of brake pad and rotor thickness and tire tread; a check of every fluid for level and condition; an inspection for signs of collision repair and structural work; and a road test. Ask for a written report with photographs and a rough estimate of what needs doing now versus soon.

How much does a pre-purchase car inspection cost in Canada?

Expect roughly $100 to $250 at an independent shop, depending on the city and how thorough the inspection is. Specialty inspections or ones that include a compression or leak-down test can cost more. Set against a used-car purchase you'll be paying for over several years, it's one of the highest-return expenditures in the whole transaction — and if the seller resists, that resistance is itself worth the price of the information.

What are the biggest red flags when buying a used car?

A seller who won't allow an independent inspection or a cold start. Mismatched VINs between the windshield, the door jamb and the paperwork. Milky or gritty engine oil, or transmission fluid that smells burnt. Perforated rust on structural areas, or corroded brake and fuel lines. A damp or musty interior, which points to a leak or flood history. Warning lights that never illuminate at all during startup, which can mean bulbs were removed. And a suspiciously steam-cleaned engine bay on an older car, which is a common way to hide active leaks.

Does a safety standards certificate mean the car is in good condition?

No, and this is a frequent and expensive misunderstanding. In Ontario, a Safety Standards Certificate confirms the vehicle met minimum safety requirements on the day it was inspected. It doesn't assess engine or transmission health, air conditioning, remaining brake or tire life beyond the minimum, or whether the car has been in a collision, and it isn't a warranty. A certificate issued weeks ago tells you even less. Treat it as a licensing document and get your own inspection regardless.

Can I get a pre-purchase inspection on a dealership car?

Usually yes, and you should ask. Many dealers will let you take a vehicle to an independent shop, or will allow a mobile inspector on site. The key word is independent — an inspection performed by the selling dealer's own service department is not the same thing, however competent they are. If a dealer refuses any outside inspection on a used vehicle, treat that as significant information about the car and about the dealer.
