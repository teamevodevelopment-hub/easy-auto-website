Financing a Car as a New Driver (Under 25) With No Credit and No Co-Signer

New Drivers · 10 min read

Most advice for young drivers focuses on getting approved for the loan. That's understandable and slightly misdirected, because for a lot of Canadians under 25 the loan turns out to be the easier half. The number that decides what you can actually afford is usually the insurance premium — and it's the one nobody warns you about until you've already picked a car.

Here's the whole picture: how lenders read a file with no credit history, what going without a co-signer actually costs you, and why the insurance quote should come before the car.

📌 Key takeaways
  • No credit is not bad credit. Lenders have programs for empty files and weigh income and stability instead.
  • In Ontario and Quebec you can enter a financing contract at 18, since that's the age of majority in both.
  • Insurance often costs more per month than the loan for a driver under 25. Quote it before you choose the car.
  • Never let a parent be listed as primary driver of a car you actually drive most. A denied claim isn't worth the saving.

The age question

You need to have reached the age of majority in your province for a financing agreement to be enforceable. In both Ontario and Quebec that's 18. Below it, contracts generally can't be enforced against a minor, which is why lenders simply won't write them regardless of your income.

Being eligible and being approved are different things. At 18 or 19 you'll usually have no credit file, limited employment history, and a short address history — three of the things a lender would normally look at. That doesn't stop the process, it just changes which parts of your application carry the weight.

Also relevant: your licence class. In Ontario's graduated system a G1 holder can't drive unaccompanied, so financing a car you can't legally drive alone rarely makes sense; a G2 is the practical starting point. Quebec's learner and probationary stages work similarly. Insurers rate on your licence class and how long you've held it, so the timing of your licence progression affects your premium directly.

How lenders read an empty credit file

This is the most reassuring part of the process and the least understood. No credit history is not the same as bad credit history, and lenders don't treat them the same way.

With a damaged file, a lender is looking at evidence of missed payments and has to decide whether it'll happen again. With an empty file, there's no negative evidence at all — just less information. So they substitute other things they can verify:

  • Employment tenure. Often the single most important factor. Six months at the same job is meaningfully better than three, and a year is better again. If you're within a couple of months of a milestone, waiting can genuinely change the outcome.
  • Verifiable income. Not just the amount but how provable it is. Regular deposits, consistent pay stubs, a letter of employment.
  • Time at your address. Stability is read as a proxy for reliability. Frequent moves — common at that age — read as risk.
  • Down payment. The strongest lever available to you. It reduces the lender's exposure and demonstrates saving discipline in one move.
  • The vehicle itself. A reasonable, easily-resold vehicle is better collateral than something exotic, and lenders care about that.
  • Debt-to-income. Student loans and phone contracts count. If you're carrying a lot already, the room for a car payment is smaller.

If you're a student or recent graduate specifically, there are program considerations that overlap with but differ from this. More on thin files for students and grads.

What no co-signer actually costs

Plenty of young buyers can't ask a parent to co-sign, or reasonably don't want to. Both are fine, and it's worth knowing exactly what you're giving up so you can plan for it.

Going without a co-signer typically means: a higher interest rate, a larger down payment requirement, a lower maximum amount, and a shorter list of lenders who'll consider the file. Not a wall — a narrower door.

What a co-signer would have provided is a second person legally responsible for the debt, with an established credit history the lender can assess. That's genuinely valuable to them, which is why it improves your terms.

What it would have cost your co-signer, and this is worth being honest about if you're considering asking: the loan appears on their credit report, counts against their debt-to-income ratio for anything else they want to borrow, and makes them fully liable if you can't pay. It's not a formality or a character reference — it's shared debt. Anyone asked should understand that clearly. The full picture on co-signing.

The genuine upside of doing it alone: the credit history you build is entirely yours. Two years of on-time payments on a loan in your own name is a real asset at 21, and it's the foundation for a much better rate on the next car and eventually a mortgage.

Insurance: the real obstacle

Here's the part that reorders everyone's priorities once they see the numbers.

For a newly licensed driver in a dense urban area, an own-name policy with the comprehensive and collision coverage your lender requires can cost more per month than the car loan. Not slightly more — sometimes considerably more. Families budget carefully for a $300 payment and then discover the insurance is $400.

It's driven by licensing experience rather than age as such. Collision data for newly licensed drivers is genuinely worse, and premiums reflect it. It isn't personal and there's no arguing it down — but there is a lot you can do about it:

  • Complete an approved driver training course. In Ontario, a ministry-approved Beginner Driver Education course can both shorten your G1 waiting period and earn an insurance discount. It's one of the highest-return few hundred dollars a young driver can spend.
  • Ask about telematics or usage-based programs. These monitor how you actually drive and can meaningfully reduce a young driver's premium — the category where the discount potential is largest, because you have no record to be judged on otherwise. Read the terms so you know what's being measured.
  • Shop it through a broker and directly. The spread between insurers for a young driver is enormous, much wider than for a 45-year-old with a clean record.
  • Choose your coverage deliberately. Your lender sets a floor on comprehensive and collision, but the deductible is often a choice. A higher deductible lowers the premium — pick a number you could genuinely produce.
  • Winter tires. Ontario insurers must offer a discount, and it applies to you as much as anyone. Winter tire costs and discounts.
  • Stay claim-free. Every year without an at-fault claim moves the number down, and by your mid-twenties the difference is dramatic. Nothing else pays off as reliably.
  • Never let it lapse. A gap in coverage history is expensive, and a cancellation for non-payment is worse. Why that's the one financial event that really does raise your premium.

The shortcut not to take

Because young-driver premiums are so high, a tempting arrangement circulates: register and insure the car in a parent's name, listing them as the primary driver, with the young person added as occasional — even though the young person drives it every day.

This is misrepresentation on an insurance application, sometimes called fronting, and the consequences land at the worst possible time. If there's a serious claim and the insurer establishes that the real primary driver was misdescribed, they can deny the claim or void the policy. On a financed vehicle that means no payout, a car that's destroyed, and a loan balance you still owe in full.

What is legitimate: being listed accurately. If you genuinely are an occasional driver of a household vehicle, that's what you should be listed as, and it will cost less. If you genuinely are the primary driver, list yourself that way and pay the honest premium.

There's also a long-game reason to insure in your own name once you can: time on your own policy builds your own insurance history. Years spent as an occasional driver on a parent's policy don't establish the record that eventually brings your premiums down. Paying more at 20 to pay much less at 25 is a real trade worth considering.

Choosing the car for the premium, not the price

If you take one action from this article, make it this one: get insurance quotes on three specific vehicles before you commit to any of them.

Insurers rate on make, model, trim and year using real claims data. For a young driver the spread between two cars in the same price bracket can be hundreds of dollars a month. A modest sedan or hatchback with good safety ratings and cheap parts is in a different universe from a sporty trim of the same model.

Things that push a young driver's premium up hard:

  • Performance trims, turbocharged engines, and anything marketed on acceleration.
  • Models with high theft rates in your area — worth asking your broker about specifically.
  • Expensive-to-repair vehicles, including some newer models packed with sensors in the bumpers.
  • Larger wheels, which also make your winter tire set more expensive.

None of this means buying something you dislike. It means finding out the premium before you fall for a particular car, when the information can still change your decision.

Building credit while you drive

An auto loan is an excellent credit-building instrument — it's an installment account with a fixed schedule, which is exactly the kind of history a thin file lacks. Two conditions on that:

Confirm the lender reports to the credit bureaus. Ask directly, before signing, and ask whether they report to both Equifax and TransUnion. A loan that never reports can't build anything no matter how perfectly you pay it. Why one-bureau reporting matters.

Add a second type of credit alongside it. Scoring models like a mix, and an auto loan on its own is a narrow file. Straightforward options for someone your age:

  • A secured credit card. You deposit a small amount as security and get a low limit. Keep the balance under about 30% of the limit and pay it in full each month. Six months of this is a visible improvement.
  • A student or entry-level credit card, if you qualify. Same rules apply — small balance, paid in full.
  • Bills in your own name. Canadian telecom accounts commonly report to the bureaus, so a phone plan paid on time contributes. Utilities vary.
  • Automate everything. One late payment at this stage does disproportionate damage because there's so little history to outweigh it. Set up pre-authorised payments for the car and autopay for the card.

Then check your own report every few months — it's free, and errors on thin files are unusually damaging. How to pull it for free in Canada.

A realistic 12-month plan

If you have some flexibility on timing, this sequence puts you in a much stronger position than applying cold:

Months 1–3. Open a secured credit card and use it for one small recurring expense, paid in full monthly. Get a phone plan in your own name if it isn't already. Open a separate savings account for the down payment and automate a transfer on payday.

Months 4–6. Stay in your job — tenure is doing quiet work for you. Complete an approved driver training course if you haven't. Pull your credit report and confirm your new accounts are appearing correctly.

Months 7–9. Start getting insurance quotes on realistic vehicles so you know what the premium range looks like. Progress your licence class if you're eligible. Keep saving.

Months 10–12. Apply for pre-approval to learn your ceiling. Then shop for a car below it, with an insurance quote already in hand for the specific vehicle. Why the pre-approval order matters.

Twelve months of this turns a thin, difficult application into a straightforward one — with a down payment, employment tenure, a small credit history, an insurance discount, and a realistic budget.

Being 19 with no credit isn't a disadvantage to overcome so much as a blank page to write on. The loan is available. The insurance is the expensive part, and it's temporary — every claim-free year makes it cheaper. Buy modestly, insure honestly, pay on time, and by your mid-twenties you'll have both a clean driving record and a real credit history, which is a genuinely strong place to be.

Frequently asked questions

Can you get a car loan at 18 in Canada?

In Ontario and Quebec, yes — the age of majority is 18 in both provinces, and a lender can enter an enforceable financing contract with you from that birthday. Below the age of majority, agreements generally aren't enforceable against a minor, which is why lenders won't write them. Being eligible isn't the same as being approved, though: at 18 you'll typically have no credit file and short employment history, so approvals lean heavily on verifiable income, time at your job, and how much you can put down.

Can you finance a car with no credit history and no co-signer?

Yes, and it's worth understanding that having no credit is treated differently from having bad credit. With no file there's no negative history to explain — the lender simply has less information, so they weigh what they can verify instead: how long you've been employed, what you earn, how long you've lived at your address, and your down payment. Without a co-signer you should expect a higher rate, a larger down payment requirement, and a lower ceiling on the amount. Those are the costs of going it alone, and they're survivable.

Why is car insurance so expensive for drivers under 25?

It's driven mainly by licensing experience rather than age itself — collision data for newly licensed drivers is genuinely worse, and premiums reflect it. In Ontario a young driver on their own policy in a dense urban area can pay more per month for insurance than for the car loan, which catches families off guard. The good news is that this cost falls steadily with each claim-free year, and it's the one number in this whole process most influenced by which vehicle you choose.

Is it cheaper to stay on my parents' insurance policy?

Usually yes in the short term, and it can be entirely legitimate if the description matches reality — being listed as an occasional driver on a household policy when you genuinely are one. What isn't legitimate is naming a parent as the primary driver of a vehicle you actually drive most, sometimes called fronting. That's a misrepresentation on the application and it can lead to a denied claim or a cancelled policy at the worst possible moment. There's also a long-term cost: time on your own policy builds your own insurance history, which is what eventually brings your premiums down.

What credit score do you need at 20 to buy a car?

There isn't a threshold you have to clear, because many 20-year-olds have no score at all and lenders have programs built for exactly that. If you do have a short file, a thin history with a few months of perfect payments on a small credit card reads better than no file at all, and better again than a file with a missed payment. Rather than chasing a number, focus on what's actually assessed: steady verifiable income, time at your job, and a down payment.
