How to Read a Used Car's Vehicle History Report (CARFAX Canada)

Vehicle History · 10 min read

Most people glance at a vehicle history report, see the word "clean," and move on to negotiating. That's a shame, because the report is the cheapest leverage in the entire transaction — and because "clean" is a much weaker statement than it appears. Understanding both what the report knows and what it structurally cannot know is what separates a confident used-car purchase from an expensive guess.

📌 Key takeaways
  • The report is a record trail, not an inspection. Absence of an accident record is not evidence of no accident.
  • A branded record — salvage, rebuilt, irreparable — is usually a hard stop for financing and sometimes for insurance.
  • In a private sale, an undischarged lien can follow the car to you. Search the provincial registry yourself.
  • Match the VIN on the report to the VIN physically on the car before you read anything else.

What the report is built from

A vehicle history report is a search of databases keyed to one thing: the vehicle identification number. In Canada, the records typically drawn together include:

  • Provincial registration and licensing records — where the vehicle has been registered, when, and under what status.
  • Insurance claim and total-loss data, including estimated or paid repair amounts where available.
  • Police-reported collisions.
  • Lien registrations from provincial personal property registries.
  • Import and export records, including vehicles brought in from the United States.
  • Odometer readings captured at registration renewals, inspections, and some service or auction events.
  • Stolen vehicle records and outstanding manufacturer recalls.

Notice what's on that list: things institutions wrote down. Insurers, police, licensing offices, lenders. The report is excellent at surfacing anything that touched a system. It has no opinion at all on the car's condition today.

The blind spot nobody mentions

This is the part to internalize before anything else.

Consider a collision where two drivers agree not to involve insurance. One hands the other cash, a body shop straightens the damage, and nobody files anything. No claim, no police report, no registration change. That accident does not exist as far as the report is concerned — and it may have involved structural repair.

The incentive to settle privately is strongest exactly where it matters most to you: on lower-value vehicles, where a claim can trigger a write-off, and with drivers protecting their premiums. Older, cheaper cars are the ones most likely to have unreported repair history, and they're also the ones subprime buyers are most often shopping.

So the correct reading of a clean report is: nothing bad was reported to the institutions this report queries. That's genuinely useful. It is not the same as "this car has never been damaged," and a report should never take the place of putting the car on a lift. The physical inspection checklist that covers this gap is here.

Start with the VIN, not the report

Before reading a single line, verify the report describes the car in front of you.

Find the VIN in at least two places on the vehicle: the plate visible through the base of the windshield on the driver's side, and the sticker in the driver's door jamb. Many vehicles also carry it on the firewall or under the hood. Compare all of them to the VIN printed on the report, character by character — not the last four, the whole thing.

Two things you're checking for. First, that the seller pulled the report for this car and not a similar one. Second, that the VINs on the vehicle agree with each other; a mismatch between the windshield plate and the door jamb sticker, or a jamb sticker that looks reprinted, altered, or freshly applied, is a serious problem. In that situation, don't negotiate — leave.

Also check the report's date. A report run eight months ago is missing eight months of events, including any accident that happened while the car sat on the lot or with the seller. If the report you're handed is stale, ask for a current one or run your own. It's a small cost relative to the purchase.

Branding and vehicle status

This section matters more than any other for financing, and it uses vocabulary that's easy to skim past.

A brand is a designation attached to a vehicle's registration record by a provincial authority, generally after severe damage. Broadly:

  • Normal — no brand. What you want to see.
  • Salvage — damaged enough to be written off; cannot legally be driven until repaired and passed through a structural inspection.
  • Rebuilt — was salvage, has been repaired, and passed inspection, so it can be plated and driven again.
  • Irreparable / non-repairable — can never legally return to the road. Parts or scrap only.

The precise terms, thresholds and inspection processes are set provincially, and they differ. Quebec's system through the SAAQ uses its own categories and its own severely-damaged-vehicle designations; Ontario's brands are applied under its highway traffic legislation. If you see any brand on a record, find out exactly what it means in the province where the vehicle is registered, and what it means for registering it in yours.

Why this is usually a dead end on a financed purchase: a branded vehicle's resale value is deeply reduced and hard to appraise consistently, which makes it weak collateral. Most mainstream auto lenders simply exclude branded vehicles from their programs — that's a vehicle eligibility rule, not a comment on your credit, and no amount of income fixes it. Insurance is a second obstacle: some insurers won't write full coverage on a rebuilt vehicle, and financing generally requires comprehensive and collision coverage.

A rebuilt car can be a perfectly sensible cash purchase for someone who knows exactly what they're getting and doesn't need to borrow. If you're financing, treat a brand as disqualifying and move on.

Accident and claim records

When damage records do appear, read them for three things rather than one.

The amount, relative to the car's value. A $2,000 claim on a $30,000 vehicle is a bumper and a fender. A $9,000 claim on a car now worth $12,000 tells you the vehicle was very close to being written off, and that a great deal of it was disassembled and put back together. The absolute number means little; the ratio means a lot.

Where the damage was. Reports often indicate the impact area. Front and rear damage frequently involves crumple zones designed to absorb it, and quality repairs are routine. Damage to the roof, the side structure at the B-pillar, or anything described as affecting structural or unibody components is a much more serious matter — alignment, crash performance in a future collision, and water intrusion all become live questions.

Whether airbags deployed. Deployment is a severity signal, because it means the sensors registered a significant impact. It's also expensive to restore correctly, which creates temptation to do it badly. If a report indicates deployment, an inspection specifically confirming the restraint system is intact and fault-free is essential.

One caution about estimates: a listed figure may be an initial estimate rather than the final repair cost, and some entries appear with no amount at all. Treat the amount as an indicator of scale, then have a mechanic tell you how the repair was actually done.

Odometer readings

The report lists readings captured at various points, with dates. You're looking for a plausible story.

Any reading lower than an earlier one is a red flag for a rollback or a cluster swap. It can occasionally have an innocent explanation — a replaced instrument cluster, a data entry error — but the seller needs to explain it and document it, and you should be skeptical.

A suspiciously flat stretch is the subtler version. If a car accumulated 20,000 kilometres a year for four years and then apparently travelled 3,000 in the next two, either it genuinely sat, or the readings aren't real. Ask which.

Very low overall mileage on an older car isn't automatically good news. A vehicle that sat for long periods has its own problems — dried seals, brake corrosion, aged fluids, flat-spotted tires, rodent damage to wiring. Low kilometres and a long gap in the record deserve extra inspection, not less.

Finally, cross-check the readings against the car itself. Wear on the driver's seat bolster, the steering wheel, the pedal rubbers and the shifter should be consistent with the number on the dash. A 60,000-kilometre car with a polished brake pedal and a shiny steering wheel is telling you something the report didn't.

Liens — the section that can cost you the car

If you take one practical action from this article, make it this one.

When someone finances a vehicle, the lender registers a security interest against it in a provincial registry. If the borrower sells the car without paying off that loan, the lender's claim generally attaches to the vehicle, not to the person who borrowed. Which means a buyer can pay in full, register the car, drive it for months, and then have it repossessed over a debt they never incurred — with their money gone and the seller unreachable.

Protecting yourself is cheap and takes minutes:

  • In Ontario, run a Personal Property Security Act (PPSA) search against the VIN through the provincial registry. The Used Vehicle Information Package that a private seller is required to provide also carries lien information — but run your own search as well, and run it close to the date you hand over money.
  • In Quebec, search the RDPRM (Register of Personal and Movable Real Rights) against the VIN.
  • Read the results for names and dates. A registration that appears discharged should say so. If you're unsure how to interpret what comes back, that ambiguity is itself a reason to slow down.

A bundled lien check inside a history report is a reasonable first pass, but the provincial registry is the authoritative source, and it's the one that will matter if there's ever a dispute. Run it yourself.

Buying from a registered dealer largely removes this risk, since clearing existing liens and delivering clear title is the dealer's responsibility. That's one of several protections you give up in a private sale — the full comparison is here.

Registration history, imports and road salt

The geography section is more informative than it looks.

Provincial history. A vehicle that spent its life in Ontario or Quebec has been through many salt seasons. One from British Columbia's south coast or from Alberta generally hasn't, and tends to show dramatically less corrosion underneath. That difference is worth real money and real inspection attention — rusted brake and fuel lines are expensive and are safety-inspection failures. A car recently arriving from a dry province is often a genuinely better buy for that reason alone.

United States imports. A US-import record isn't disqualifying, but it raises questions worth answering. Damage and flood history from another jurisdiction can be harder to trace fully, some equipment and disclosure standards differ, and a car imported after a write-off in its home state is a known pattern. Also check that the import was done properly and that the vehicle was admissible — that affects your ability to register it. If a car looks unusually cheap and shows an import record, dig in.

Flood and hail. These sometimes appear as claim types. Flood damage in particular has a long tail of electrical faults that surface unpredictably, and it's the kind of history that should end your interest in a car you plan to depend on for a multi-year loan.

Owners, usage type and recalls

Number of owners. Fewer isn't automatically better, but a car with five owners in six years is worth asking about — that pattern often means people kept discovering something.

Declared usage. Reports may flag prior use as a daily rental, taxi, limousine, police, or fleet vehicle. None of these is automatically ruinous. Fleet vehicles are often maintained on strict schedules; rentals are usually serviced but hard-driven by many drivers. The point is that declared prior use should be reflected in the price, and in Ontario it's among the things a registered dealer is required to disclose.

Worth knowing: use as a rideshare vehicle typically doesn't appear anywhere. A car that spent three years doing high-mileage city driving for a platform looks like an ordinary private vehicle on paper. High kilometres relative to the vehicle's age, combined with heavy interior wear and a service history full of brakes and tires, is the pattern to notice.

Recalls. Reports flag outstanding recalls, but check independently as well — Transport Canada's recall database is free and searchable, and manufacturers will generally look up open recalls by VIN at no charge. Outstanding recall work is usually free to remedy, so this is pure upside: find it, then have it done.

How the report affects your financing

The report isn't only about whether you want the car. It affects whether the deal can be funded.

  • Brands are typically disqualifying. Salvage, rebuilt and non-repairable vehicles fall outside most lender programs regardless of credit.
  • Significant damage history can affect the appraised value, and therefore the amount a lender will advance. If the loan is sized to the vehicle's value, a heavy accident record can leave a funding gap you'd have to cover in cash.
  • Age and kilometres interact with eligibility rules. A verified odometer reading close to a program's limit can put the deal outside it. More on how vehicle age drives approvals and rates.
  • An unresolved lien stops a private-sale deal cold, because no lender will fund a purchase where someone else holds a prior claim on the collateral.

Sorting the vehicle question early saves the frustrating version of this process, where an approval is in hand and then a specific car turns out to be ineligible.

Your reading checklist

  • ✓Match the VIN on the report to the windshield plate and the door jamb sticker, in full.
  • ✓Check the report date. Stale reports miss recent events — get a current one.
  • ✓Look for any brand first. If one exists, stop and find out what it means before going further.
  • ✓Compare claim amounts to the car's current value, not to each other.
  • ✓Read the impact locations and treat structural or side-impact damage differently from bumper work.
  • ✓Trace the odometer sequence in order, watching for decreases and implausibly flat stretches.
  • ✓Run your own PPSA or RDPRM lien search, close to the day you pay.
  • ✓Note every province the car has lived in, and inspect the underside accordingly.
  • ✓Check open recalls independently through Transport Canada or the manufacturer.
  • ✓Then book an independent inspection anyway. The report and the inspection answer different questions.

A vehicle history report is one of the few places in car buying where a small amount of money and twenty minutes of attention can save you thousands. Read it as a record of what institutions noticed — thorough on titles, claims and liens, silent on cash repairs and current condition — and pair it with a mechanic who can see what the databases can't.

Frequently asked questions

What does a CARFAX Canada report actually show?

It aggregates records tied to a vehicle's VIN from sources including provincial registration and licensing bodies, insurance claim and total-loss data, police-reported collisions, lien registries, import and export records, and odometer readings captured at registration or service events. Reports also flag stolen-vehicle records and outstanding manufacturer recalls. What you get is a record trail, not an inspection — it tells you what was reported to somebody, and nothing about the car's current mechanical condition.

Can a car have accident damage that doesn't appear on a vehicle history report?

Yes, and this is the single most important limitation to understand. If a collision was never claimed through insurance and never attended by police — the two parties settled in cash and a body shop did the work privately — there is usually no record for the report to find. Structural repairs can be invisible in the data. A report with no accidents means no accidents were reported, not that none happened, which is why a clean report never replaces a physical inspection.

What's the difference between a salvage and rebuilt vehicle in Canada?

These are provincial brands applied to a vehicle's registration record. Broadly, salvage means the vehicle was damaged enough to be written off and cannot legally be driven until it's repaired and passes a structural inspection. Once it passes, the brand typically changes to rebuilt, and it can be plated again. Irreparable or non-repairable means it can never return to the road and may only be used for parts or scrap. Exact terminology and process vary by province, so confirm what the specific brand means where the vehicle is registered.

Can you finance a rebuilt or salvage title car in Canada?

Usually not through mainstream auto lenders. A branded vehicle is poor collateral — its resale value is heavily reduced and difficult to appraise reliably — so most finance programs exclude branded vehicles outright, regardless of the borrower's credit. Insurance can also be harder to place, and some insurers won't write full coverage, which is a problem because financing normally requires it. If a listing is priced well below comparable vehicles, a brand on the record is one of the first things to check.

How do I check if a used car has a lien on it?

Search the provincial registry directly rather than relying only on a bundled report. In Ontario that's a Personal Property Security Act search; in Quebec it's the RDPRM. This matters most in private sales: if the seller still owes money secured against the vehicle and doesn't pay it off, the lender's claim generally follows the car rather than the seller — meaning it can be repossessed from you after you've paid for it. Buying through a registered dealer normally shifts responsibility for clearing any lien to the dealer.
