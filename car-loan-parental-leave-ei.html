Getting a Car Loan While on Parental Leave or EI

Parental Leave · 10 min read

Needing a car during parental leave is one of those situations where the timing feels almost designed to be difficult. Your income has dropped, your expenses have gone up, and suddenly the vehicle that was optional last year has become the thing that gets you to a paediatric appointment across town.

It is doable. The process is different from an ordinary application, though, and knowing what an underwriter is actually worried about — plus which single document addresses it — makes a large difference to how the conversation goes.

📌 Key takeaways
  • Lenders treat parental leave benefits very differently from one another. A decline is often a program rule, not a verdict.
  • An employer letter confirming your position, return date and returning salary is the most valuable page in your file.
  • Parental benefits with a job to return to read far better than regular EI after a layoff.
  • Budget on your leave income, not your returning salary — even if you're approved on the latter.

What the underwriter is actually worried about

It's not that your income is lower. Lenders finance people with modest incomes constantly. The concern is temporariness.

Parental leave benefits are designed to end. An underwriter looking at a 60- or 72-month loan against a benefit that stops in eleven months is looking at a mismatch, and their question is simply: what happens to this household's income when the benefit stops?

Once you see it that way, the whole application becomes easier to prepare for. You're not trying to make your leave income look bigger. You're trying to document what comes after it. Applicants who can do that convincingly are frequently assessed on their returning salary rather than on the benefit amount — which is an entirely different approval.

The document that changes the conversation

Ask your employer for a letter, on company letterhead, that states:

  • Your position and title, and that your employment is continuing.
  • Your confirmed return-to-work date.
  • Your salary or hourly rate and hours on return.
  • Your original start date with the employer, which establishes tenure.
  • A contact name and phone number in HR or payroll for verification.

That's it. One page. Most HR departments produce these routinely and will turn it around in a few days if you ask plainly — "I'm applying for financing and need a letter confirming my position, return date and salary on return" is all it takes.

Why it carries so much weight: it converts "this applicant is on a benefit that expires" into "this applicant has an employer, a confirmed return date, and a documented salary." Some lenders will underwrite directly to that figure. Others will use it as supporting strength alongside the benefit income. Either way it moves your file up.

If your return date isn't fixed yet, ask for the letter to state your position, your continuing employment, your salary, and the expected return window. Less powerful than a firm date, still far better than nothing.

Parental benefits vs. regular EI

These get lumped together as "EI" and they read very differently to a lender. Worth separating.

Maternity and parental benefits. You have an employer. Your job is being held. There's a planned end and employment on the other side of it. Federal EI provides maternity benefits for the person giving birth for a period of weeks, plus parental benefits that can be shared between parents — with a standard option paying a higher weekly rate over a shorter period and an extended option paying a lower rate over a longer one. The choice you made between those affects both your monthly amount and how long it lasts, which is exactly what a lender wants to see documented.

Regular EI after a job loss. No employer, no return date, no confirmed income on the other side. Understandably a much harder file, and many lenders won't count it as qualifying income at all. If that's your situation, the realistic paths are a co-applicant with employment income, a substantial down payment, or waiting until you're working again. Not what anyone wants to hear, but it saves you a series of declines.

One useful detail if you're choosing between the standard and extended parental options and know you'll need to finance something: the standard option pays a higher monthly amount over a shorter period. If you're going to apply during your leave, the higher monthly figure will present better — though obviously that shouldn't be the main basis for a decision about time with your child.

Quebec: QPIP instead of EI

If you live in Quebec, your maternity, paternity and parental benefits come through the Quebec Parental Insurance Plan rather than federal EI. It's a distinct provincial program with its own structure, and a few features matter here:

  • Plan options. Quebec offers a choice between options that pay a larger share of your earnings over a shorter period and options that pay less over longer. Your benefit statement will show which you chose and the weekly amount — bring it.
  • Paternity benefits are provided as a distinct category, which is more generous than the federal structure and means both parents' documentation may be relevant to a household application.
  • Self-employed coverage. QPIP covers self-employed workers, which is a meaningful difference for Quebec applicants who run their own business. If that's you, combine your QPIP statement with your business documentation. What lenders want to see from self-employed applicants.

Most lenders active in Quebec understand the program perfectly well. Occasionally you'll deal with someone who doesn't, and the fix is simply to have the benefit statement in hand showing the plan and the amount rather than trying to explain it verbally. Other ways the two provinces differ.

Employer top-ups

If your employer supplements your benefits — a top-up arrangement that brings you closer to your regular pay for part or all of the leave — document it and present it. It's real, verifiable income and it directly raises the figure a lender is working with.

What to gather: the written policy or your leave agreement showing the top-up percentage and duration, and pay stubs or deposit records showing it actually arriving. A top-up mentioned verbally is worth much less than a top-up on paper.

Ask HR specifically for confirmation of the top-up amount and how long it runs, ideally in the same letter as your return-to-work confirmation. One document, two purposes.

Other ways to strengthen the file

Beyond the employer letter, these are the levers that genuinely move a leave-period application:

  • Apply with a co-applicant if there's a second income. A partner with employment income transforms the household picture. Both incomes get counted, and the temporariness problem largely disappears. Understand that it's joint liability, not a formality. How co-applicants and co-signers differ.
  • Increase the down payment. It lowers the amount financed, the payment, and the lender's exposure all at once. On a file with a temporary income question, this is the most reliable single improvement. How much actually helps.
  • Choose a smaller payment than you'd otherwise consider. A lender assessing a temporary income is far more comfortable with a modest payment. So should you be.
  • Bring pre-leave pay stubs and your last T4. They establish what your normal earnings look like and corroborate the employer letter.
  • Bring 90 days of bank statements showing the benefit deposits landing. Consistency of deposits is persuasive.
  • Clear up small debts first if you can. Reducing other monthly obligations creates room without needing more income.
  • Check your credit report before applying so nothing unexpected surfaces mid-process. How to pull it free.

Budget on the leave, not the return

This is the part that matters most for your actual life, and it's separate from approval.

If you're approved on the strength of your returning salary, congratulations — but you still have to make payments during the leave, at leave income. A payment that's comfortable on your full salary can be genuinely difficult on 55% of it, and the months where it's difficult are the months you're most stretched in every other way.

So do the arithmetic on the harder version: your actual monthly benefit, plus any top-up, minus your existing bills, minus new baby costs, minus the total cost of the car — not just the payment, but insurance, fuel and everything else. How to build that total properly.

If it's tight, buy less car. A payment $120 lower is not a lesser vehicle in any meaningful sense; it's a vehicle that doesn't create a monthly crisis. And there's a credit dimension too — a missed payment during leave stays on your file for about six years, which is a very expensive way to have stretched.

The tax bill nobody mentions

A practical warning that catches many families in their first spring after a leave.

Parental leave benefits are taxable income, and the tax withheld at source is often less than what you'll ultimately owe — particularly if you had employment income earlier in the same tax year, or if you received both benefits and an employer top-up, or if there were two income sources. The result is a balance owing at tax time in a year when money is already tight.

Two things to do about it: set aside a small amount monthly against it if you can, and don't let a surprise tax bill turn into a missed car payment. If it does happen, contact your lender before the payment date rather than after — options exist when you call early and almost none exist when you call late.

If you can time it, time it

Where there's flexibility, timing is the single biggest advantage available.

Arranging financing before your leave starts — while you're at full salary with ordinary pay stubs — turns a complicated application into a routine one. If you know a leave is coming and you also know the car is going to be needed, doing it in that order is dramatically easier. Not everyone gets that choice, but if you do, take it.

The second-best timing is early in the leave, while your recent pay stubs still reflect full employment income and your return date is comfortably documented.

The hardest timing is late in a long leave, with your last full pay stub over a year old and the return date approaching but not yet arrived. Still workable with a good employer letter, just more friction.

Frequently asked questions

Can you get a car loan while on maternity or parental leave in Canada?

Yes, and it's more common than people expect — but it depends heavily on which lender reviews your file. The complication is that parental leave benefits are temporary by design, so an underwriter is really asking what your income looks like after the leave ends. Applicants who can document a job to return to, with a confirmed position and salary, are often assessed on that returning income rather than on the benefit amount. Without that documentation, some lenders will discount or exclude the benefit entirely.

Does EI count as income for a car loan?

Sometimes fully, sometimes partially, sometimes not at all — treatment varies more between lenders on this than on almost any other income type. It also depends which EI benefit you're receiving. Maternity and parental benefits, where you have an employer and a return date, are viewed far more favourably than regular EI following a layoff, because one has a defined end with employment on the other side and the other doesn't. If a lender declines on this basis, it reflects that program's rules rather than a judgment about your finances.

Is Quebec's parental leave treated differently by lenders?

Quebec residents receive maternity, paternity and parental benefits through the Quebec Parental Insurance Plan rather than federal EI, and the plan has its own structure, including options with a higher share of your earnings over a shorter period. Well-informed lenders assess it the same way they'd assess EI parental benefits, but you may occasionally need to explain what the program is. Bring your benefit statement showing the plan option and payment amount, and it becomes a straightforward conversation.

What documents do I need to get approved while on parental leave?

The most valuable single document is a letter from your employer on company letterhead confirming your position, your confirmed return-to-work date, and your salary on return. That one page can change how your entire file is assessed. Alongside it, bring your benefit statement showing the payment amount and duration, documentation of any employer top-up, pay stubs from before your leave began, recent bank statements showing the deposits, and your most recent T4. If there's a second income in the household, bring their documents too.

Should I wait until I am back at work to finance a car?

If you can wait, it's generally the easier route — a full salary with no explanation required is the simplest file an underwriter can see. But that's not always realistic, and waiting has real costs if you need a car for medical appointments, childcare or work. If you do need to buy during leave, the important discipline is to budget on your leave income rather than your returning salary, even if you're approved on the latter. Being approved for a payment you can only afford after the leave ends means several months of strain during a period that's already demanding.
