How Winter Tires Affect Your Insurance and Financing in Canada

Winter Driving · 9 min read

Winter tires get discussed in Canada as though the only question is whether you believe in them. The more useful conversation is about money: what a set actually costs over its life, what the insurance discount is genuinely worth, and — the part that catches people out — why letting the dealer roll them into your car loan is one of the more expensive ways to buy tires.

📌 Key takeaways
  • Quebec requires compliant winter tires by law for a defined period each winter. Ontario doesn't, but requires insurers to offer a discount.
  • Look for the three-peaked mountain and snowflake marking. "M+S" alone is not a winter tire.
  • The insurance discount won't pay for the tires. The avoided at-fault claim will, several times over.
  • Financing a $1,600 tire package at 18% over 60 months costs well over $2,400. Buy them separately.

The law: Quebec and Ontario

Quebec. Winter tires are mandatory on passenger vehicles registered in the province for a defined stretch of the winter — currently from the start of December to the middle of March. Driving without compliant tires during that window carries a fine, and the tires have to carry the recognised winter designation rather than simply being marked for mud and snow. Rental vehicles and certain categories have their own rules. Because the dates have been adjusted in the past, it's worth confirming the current period with the SAAQ each fall rather than relying on memory.

Ontario. No general requirement for passenger vehicles. What Ontario has instead is an insurance rule: since the beginning of 2016, auto insurers in the province have been required to offer a winter tire discount to customers who install them. Note the word "offer" — in practice you usually have to tell your insurer, and sometimes provide proof, before it's applied.

If you drive between the two provinces in winter — which plenty of people in Ottawa, Gatineau and along the corridor do routinely — the Quebec requirement applies to Quebec-registered vehicles, but an Ontario-plated car driving into Quebec should still be equipped for the conditions it's actually going to meet. More on how the two provinces differ.

The marking that actually matters

This trips up a lot of buyers, and tire shops don't always volunteer it.

"M+S" (mud and snow) stamped on a sidewall describes a tread pattern. It is not a performance standard, involves no low-temperature testing, and appears on plenty of ordinary all-season tires. On its own, M+S does not make a tire a winter tire.

The three-peaked mountain with a snowflake inside it is the marking that counts. A tire carrying it has met a defined snow-traction test. This is the symbol Quebec's requirement is built around and the one your insurer will look for.

When you're shopping, look at the sidewall or ask the retailer to confirm the symbol is present. If a set is being described as "winter rated" without it, that's worth questioning.

Why it's about temperature, not snow

The common objection is "I don't need winters, I drive in the city and the roads get plowed." It misunderstands what the tire is doing.

The main difference isn't tread pattern — it's rubber compound. All-season rubber is formulated for mild conditions and stiffens as the temperature drops toward and below freezing. A stiff tire can't conform to the road surface, so it grips less. That happens on cold, dry, bare pavement, not just on snow.

Which means the benefit shows up in ordinary situations: stopping distance at a red light on a clear January morning, grip on an off-ramp at minus fifteen, and control on the black ice that forms at dusk after a wet afternoon. Plowed roads don't remove the temperature.

The practical rule most Canadian drivers use: install them when daytime temperatures are consistently at or below about seven degrees, and take them off when spring settles. Waiting for the first snowfall means every tire shop in your city is booked for two weeks.

What the insurance discount is really worth

Let's put an honest number on it rather than treating it as a headline benefit.

Ontario winter tire discounts typically run in the low single digits as a percentage of premium. On a $2,000 annual premium, a few percent is somewhere around $50 to $100 a year. Over five years, call it $250 to $500.

A set of tires and rims will cost more than that. So if the discount is your entire justification, the math doesn't work. Anyone telling you winter tires pay for themselves through the insurance discount alone is overselling.

What the discount is genuinely good for: it's free money you're probably not claiming. If you already run winter tires and haven't told your insurer, that's a phone call worth making today. And when you're comparing quotes, ask each insurer what their winter tire discount is, because they differ. The full list of discounts worth asking for by name.

The bigger insurance argument

Here's where the real money is, and it's about the claim you don't make.

An at-fault collision is the single largest lever on a Canadian auto premium. One at-fault claim can raise your rates substantially and keep them elevated for years — an effect that typically dwarfs any discount. For a driver already paying a high premium, it can be the difference between affordable and not.

Then there's the financing dimension, which is worse. If a winter collision writes off a financed vehicle, your insurer pays what the car was worth, and you still owe the loan balance. On a subprime loan with little down, that shortfall can be thousands of dollars — for a car you no longer have, while you're also trying to arrange transportation. The gap coverage math.

Set against that, a tire set is inexpensive risk reduction. Not a guarantee — winter tires don't repeal physics, and drivers who over-trust them are a known hazard — but a real and measurable improvement in stopping and cornering when it's cold.

What a set actually costs over five years

Budget honestly, including the parts people forget:

  • Four winter tires: commonly $600 to $1,200 depending on size and brand. Larger wheels on newer vehicles push this up quickly, which is worth remembering when choosing a trim level.
  • Steel rims: roughly $400 to $600 for a set. Optional, but see below — they usually save money overall.
  • TPMS sensors, if your vehicle requires them on the second set. These can add meaningfully per wheel, and it's a cost people discover at the counter.
  • Changeovers: twice a year. Much cheaper if the tires are already on rims, since it's a wheel swap rather than dismounting and rebalancing.
  • Storage: a seasonal fee if you don't have space at home.

Why rims usually pay for themselves. Mounting and balancing tires onto the same set of rims twice a year costs more per visit, takes longer, and puts wear on the tire beads every single time. On a dedicated set of rims the seasonal swap is quick and cheap. Over four or five winters, the rims typically come out ahead — and your tires last longer.

The offset nobody counts. Running winters for four or five months a year means your all-seasons aren't wearing during that period. Your summer set lasts noticeably longer, so the second set isn't a pure addition to your costs. It's still a real expense, just less than the sticker suggests. Why not to finance them with the car

Many dealers will offer to include a tire and rim package in the amount financed. It sounds painless — a few dollars a month — and it's one of the more expensive ways to buy tires.

Run the numbers. A $1,600 package added to a 60-month loan at 18% works out to about $41 a month. Over the full term that's roughly $2,440. You paid about $840 in interest on a set of tires that will be worn out before the loan is finished.

Better options, in rough order:

  • Pay for them separately, even if it means waiting a couple of paycheques into the season.
  • Use a tire retailer's short-term payment plan, if it's genuinely interest-free and you'll clear it within the promotional period. Read what happens if you don't.
  • Buy a set used or on last season's clearance. Late winter and early spring are when good tires get cheap. Check the DOT date code and the remaining tread depth — winter tires are generally considered finished for winter use well before they reach the legal minimum, because the deep tread is the point.
  • Consider all-weather tires instead, which remove the second set entirely. More on that below.
  • Negotiate them into the deal as an included item rather than as an additional financed amount. That's a price negotiation, not a financing one, and it's a reasonable thing to ask for.

The general principle applies beyond tires: anything with a short life is a poor thing to finance over a long term. The same logic covers protection packages and accessories offered in the finance office.

All-weather tires as a middle path

Underdiscussed, and genuinely the right answer for some people.

All-weather tires are a distinct category from all-seasons. They carry the three-peaked mountain and snowflake designation — so they satisfy Quebec's requirement and generally qualify for Ontario's discount — while being built to run year-round without swapping.

They make sense if: you have nowhere to store a second set, you don't want to pay for changeovers twice a year, you drive mostly in a city that gets plowed promptly, or your winters are milder than the Canadian average.

Dedicated winters are still better if: you're regularly in deep cold, you drive rural or unplowed roads, you commute long distances through the winter, or you're in a region that gets serious lake-effect snow.

The trade-offs are real in both directions: all-weather tires don't match a dedicated winter tire in severe conditions, and they wear faster than all-seasons in summer heat, so you'll replace them sooner. But for someone in a condo with no storage and a tight budget, one set of properly winter-rated tires beats a set of all-seasons and a plan to buy winters "next year."

Doing this the cheap way

  • ✓Check the sidewall for the mountain-and-snowflake symbol before buying anything described as a winter tire.
  • ✓Tell your insurer you have them, and ask for the discount by name. It's frequently not applied automatically.
  • ✓Buy them on their own steel rims. Cheaper swaps, less tire damage, faster appointments.
  • ✓Ask whether your vehicle needs TPMS sensors on the second set before you budget.
  • ✓Don't finance them into your car loan. Ask for the package to be included in the negotiated price instead.
  • ✓Shop late-season clearance in February and March for next winter, and check DOT date codes.
  • ✓Book the changeover in October, before the first snowfall books out every shop in the city.
  • ✓Consider all-weather tires if storage or changeover costs are the real obstacle.
  • ✓Check tire pressure monthly through winter. Cold air drops pressure, and underinflated tires wear out early and grip worse.
  • ✓Factor tire size into your vehicle choice. Larger wheels on a higher trim mean a much more expensive winter set every few years.

The summary: buy them for the safety and the avoided claim, claim the discount because it's there, and pay for them outside your car loan. That last point is the one that saves the most money and gets mentioned the least.

Leave room in the budget for the second set

Frequently asked questions

Are winter tires mandatory in Ontario?

No. Ontario has no law requiring winter tires on passenger vehicles, though some highways post seasonal requirements for certain vehicle types and conditions. What Ontario does have is a rule on the insurance side: since January 2016, auto insurers operating in the province have been required to offer a winter tire discount to policyholders who use them. The discount is usually small in percentage terms, and it is not always applied automatically — you generally have to tell your insurer you've installed them.

Are winter tires mandatory in Quebec?

Yes. Passenger vehicles registered in Quebec must be equipped with compliant winter tires for a defined period each winter, currently running from the beginning of December through mid-March, and driving without them carries a fine. The tires must carry the recognised winter designation rather than simply being labelled M+S. Confirm the current dates and any exemptions with the SAAQ before the season starts, since the requirement has been adjusted before.

Do winter tires lower your car insurance in Canada?

In Ontario, yes — insurers must offer a discount, though it's typically a modest percentage rather than a dramatic saving. On a $2,000 annual premium a few percent works out to roughly $50 to $100 a year, which won't pay for the tires by itself. The larger financial benefit is indirect: avoiding a single at-fault winter collision protects you from a premium increase that follows you for years, and from the much bigger problem of a written-off vehicle you still owe money on.

Should I finance winter tires with my car loan?

Try hard not to. Dealers often offer to add a tire and rim package to the amount financed, which is convenient but means paying your loan's interest rate on tires for the entire term. A $1,600 package rolled into a 60-month loan at 18% ends up costing well over $2,400 — you're paying roughly half again for the convenience. Buying them separately, even on a short-term plan from a tire retailer, is almost always cheaper. If cash flow makes that impossible, buy them next season rather than financing them for five years.

What is the difference between all-season and all-weather tires?

All-season tires are a compromise designed for mild conditions; their rubber stiffens as temperatures fall toward freezing, which reduces grip even on dry pavement. All-weather tires are a distinct category that carries the three-peaked mountain and snowflake winter designation while remaining suitable to run year-round, so they satisfy Quebec's requirement and generally qualify for Ontario's discount without seasonal swapping. They're not quite as capable as dedicated winter tires in severe cold and they wear faster in summer heat, but for drivers with nowhere to store a second set they're a genuinely sensible middle option.
