Car Insurance With Bad Credit: Does Your Score Actually Affect Your Rate?

Insurance · 9 min read

If you've been reading about credit and car insurance, there's a good chance most of what you found was written for an American audience — where insurers routinely use a credit-based insurance score, and a low score really can mean a much higher premium.

Canada works differently, and in Ontario it works very differently. Here's the honest picture of what your credit does and doesn't do to your auto premium, what's actually driving the number you were quoted, and the one money-related thing that genuinely will make your insurance more expensive.

📌 Key takeaways
  • In Ontario, auto insurers are not permitted to use credit history or credit scoring to rate or underwrite your policy.
  • Your driving record, years licensed, postal code and vehicle do the heavy lifting on your premium.
  • Financing raises your insurance because the lender requires comprehensive and collision — not because of the loan itself.
  • A cancellation for non-payment is the money problem that genuinely does raise your rates, for years.

The short answer, by province

For automobile insurance in Ontario, your credit score does not affect your rate. Ontario sets out which risk classification factors insurers may use, and credit history isn't one of them. An insurer can't quote you higher because your score is 540, and can't decline you on that basis either.

Across the rest of the country the picture varies, but the direction is consistent: several provinces restrict or prohibit the use of credit information in auto insurance, and in British Columbia, Saskatchewan and Manitoba the basic coverage comes from a public insurer that doesn't rate on credit at all. Where credit information is permitted for insurance, it's usually with your consent and more often in home and property lines than in auto.

So if a quote came back painfully high and you assumed your credit was the reason, that's almost certainly not what happened. Something else on your file is driving it, and it's worth finding out what — because unlike your credit score, some of those things you can act on immediately.

Why Ontario is different

Ontario's auto insurance market is unusually tightly regulated. Rates and the underwriting rules insurers apply are supervised by the province's financial services regulator, and insurers file the factors and rating structures they intend to use. The set of permitted classification factors is defined, and credit isn't in it.

There's a policy reason behind this that's worth understanding, because it explains why the rule is stable rather than an accident. Credit-based insurance rating tends to charge more to people who are already under financial strain — the same people for whom losing a vehicle is most damaging. Ontario's framework takes the position that auto insurance, which is compulsory, shouldn't be priced on financial hardship.

The practical takeaway for you: if a broker or agent ever suggests your Ontario auto quote is high "because of your credit," that's wrong, and it's worth asking them to walk you through the actual rating factors instead.

How Quebec's system works

Quebec splits auto insurance in two, which surprises people moving from other provinces.

Bodily injury is public. The Société de l'assurance automobile du Québec covers injuries to people in a vehicle accident, on a no-fault basis, funded through licence and registration fees. You don't shop for it and you don't get rated on it as an individual risk in the way private insurance works.

Property damage and civil liability are private. You buy that from a private insurer, and the sector is overseen by the Autorité des marchés financiers. This is the part where rating factors, deductibles and coverage choices come into play — and the part your lender will care about, since it's where collision and comprehensive coverage live.

Practices around credit information in the private portion are not identical to Ontario's outright prohibition, so the honest advice for a Quebec reader is to ask directly: does this insurer use credit information in any part of the quote, and can I decline without losing coverage? A straightforward question, and you're entitled to a straight answer. More on where the two provinces diverge on car loans generally.

What actually drives your premium

If it isn't credit, what is it? Roughly in order of impact:

  • Your driving record and claims history. At-fault claims and convictions are the biggest single lever, and they follow you for years. One at-fault collision can change a premium more than every other factor combined.
  • How long you've been licensed. Not your age exactly — your licensing experience. This is why a 30-year-old who got their licence last year pays like a new driver. More on what new drivers face.
  • Where you park it overnight. Rating territory is postal-code based, reflecting local claim frequency, theft rates and repair costs. Moving across a city boundary can change a premium meaningfully.
  • The exact vehicle. Not just make and model but trim and year, based on real claims data — repair cost, theft attractiveness, and how the vehicle performs in collisions. Two trims of the same model can rate differently.
  • How much you drive and what for. Annual kilometres, and whether the car is used for commuting, pleasure, or business.
  • Coverage and deductibles. What you carry and how much you self-insure through the deductible.
  • Continuity. A gap in your coverage history is a red flag to insurers and pushes quotes up.

Notice how many of these are about the vehicle rather than about you. That's the most actionable thing in this article: choosing a different car can change your premium more than almost anything else you control.

Why the financed car costs more to insure

Here's the connection people actually feel, and it isn't about credit at all.

When you finance, the lender registers security against the vehicle. Their collateral is the car, so they require you to protect it: comprehensive and collision coverage, usually with a cap on the deductible, and they'll require being named as loss payee on the policy so any claim payment involving the vehicle flows through them.

If your previous car was an old paid-off vehicle running liability-only, this is a genuine step up in cost, and it has nothing to do with your loan terms or your score. It's the coverage that changed.

Two practical consequences:

  • Get insurance quotes before you choose the vehicle, not after you sign. The premium difference between two cars in the same price range can be hundreds of dollars a year, and it's a cost you carry for the whole term.
  • Understand what the insurer would actually pay if the car were written off. Most policies pay actual cash value at the time of loss, which on a financed vehicle can be less than your loan balance. That shortfall is what gap coverage exists to handle.

The credit-adjacent risk that is real

There is one way financial trouble reliably makes your insurance more expensive in Canada, and it catches people out precisely because they were told credit doesn't matter.

Missing insurance payments. Most people pay monthly. Miss one and you'll get a notice; keep missing and the policy is cancelled for non-payment. A cancellation for non-payment is recorded and visible to other insurers, and its effects are serious:

  • Many standard insurers won't write a new policy for someone with a recent non-payment cancellation, which pushes you into the high-risk market at a much higher premium.
  • The effect persists for years, long after the original missed payment is settled.
  • You may be required to pay annually up front rather than monthly, which is a large cash demand at exactly the wrong moment.
  • It can breach your loan agreement. Your lender requires continuous coverage. A lapse can trigger the lender placing its own expensive coverage on the vehicle and adding the cost to your loan, or in serious cases put you in default.

The defence is simple and worth acting on: if you know a payment is going to be short, phone your insurer before the withdrawal date. Insurers can often move a date or restructure a plan for someone who calls ahead. They can do very little for someone who calls after the cancellation notice.

Legitimate ways to lower the number

None of these involve your credit score, and all of them are available to you today.

  • Shop it properly, through more than one channel. Rates for the same driver vary a lot between insurers. A broker can quote several companies at once; direct insurers quote only themselves. Do both.
  • Ask for every discount by name. Multi-vehicle, multi-line (bundling home or tenant insurance), winter tires in Ontario, telematics or usage-based programs, retiree, alumni and professional association, and paying annually instead of monthly. Discounts are frequently not applied unless you ask.
  • Put winter tires on. Ontario insurers are required to offer a winter tire discount, and the safety benefit is separate from the money. How that discount and the cost actually compare.
  • Reconsider the vehicle before you buy. Higher-performance trims and frequently-stolen models cost real money every month. Ask for a quote on your shortlist.
  • Review your deductible. Raising it lowers your premium — but only raise it to a number you could actually produce on short notice, and confirm your lender permits it.
  • Check your record for errors. Convictions and claims that should have aged off sometimes haven't. Ask your insurer what's on your record and challenge anything that looks wrong.
  • Don't let coverage lapse, ever. Continuity is worth more than most discounts.

Before you buy the policy

  • ✓Quote insurance on your shortlist before you choose the car, not after. Two similar vehicles can differ by hundreds a year.
  • ✓Tell the insurer the car will be financed so the quote includes comprehensive and collision and the loss payee.
  • ✓Ask what deductible your lender allows before choosing one.
  • ✓Get quotes from a broker and at least one direct insurer. They rarely land in the same place.
  • ✓Read out the discount list and ask which apply. Don't wait to be offered them.
  • ✓Ask what your record shows, and challenge any claim or conviction that should have aged off.
  • ✓Check whether monthly payments carry a service charge, and what paying annually would save.
  • ✓Confirm the coverage start date lines up with delivery. You can't drive off the lot uninsured.
  • ✓Set a calendar reminder before each withdrawal date while money is tight. A phone call beforehand costs nothing; a cancellation costs years.

The reassuring part of all this is that the thing you were most worried about isn't the problem. Your score doesn't set your Ontario auto premium. The things that do — the car you pick, the coverage you carry, the discounts you claim, and never letting the policy lapse — are mostly things you can still influence right now.

Frequently asked questions

Does bad credit affect car insurance rates in Ontario?

No. Ontario regulates which factors auto insurers are allowed to use when classifying risk and setting premiums, and credit history and credit scoring are not among them. An insurer operating in Ontario cannot quote you a higher auto premium because your score is low, or refuse to insure you for that reason. This is a meaningful difference from the United States, where credit-based insurance scoring is widespread — so American advice about credit and car insurance doesn't transfer.

Do Canadian insurers check your credit at all?

It depends on the province and the type of insurance. For auto, several provinces restrict or prohibit the use of credit information, and the basic coverage sold by public insurers in British Columbia, Saskatchewan and Manitoba doesn't use it. Home and property insurance is a different story — in some provinces insurers may use credit information with your consent. If you're ever asked to consent to a credit check for insurance, ask what it's used for and whether you can decline without losing the quote.

Why is my car insurance so expensive if it's not my credit?

The heavyweight factors are your driving record and claims history, how long you've been licensed, your postal code, and the specific vehicle. Two people on the same street with the same car can pay very different premiums because one has an at-fault claim from three years ago. Coverage choices matter too — financing a vehicle means carrying comprehensive and collision, which costs considerably more than liability alone. A gap in your coverage history or a past cancellation will also push a quote up sharply.

Does financing a car increase your insurance premium?

Indirectly, yes. Your lender holds security in the vehicle and will require you to carry comprehensive and collision coverage and to name them as loss payee, so you can't run the minimum liability-only policy that an owner of an old paid-off car might choose. That's usually the single largest reason a financed vehicle costs more to insure than the car it replaced. The financing itself doesn't change your rating — the required coverage does.

What happens if I miss a car insurance payment in Canada?

This is where money trouble genuinely does raise your insurance cost. A missed payment typically triggers a notice, and continued non-payment can lead to cancellation. A cancellation for non-payment stays visible to insurers for years, makes you much harder to place, and often pushes you into the high-risk market at a substantially higher premium. It can also breach your loan agreement, since your lender requires continuous coverage. If you're going to be short, call your insurer before the payment date rather than after.
