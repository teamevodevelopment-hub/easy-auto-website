VIN Checks and Recalls: What to Verify Before You Sign Anything

Before You Sign · 10 min read

Seventeen characters on a small plate at the base of the windshield contain more verifiable truth about a used car than anything the seller will tell you. The VIN tells you where the vehicle was built, what model year it actually is, whether anyone has interfered with the number, whether there's money owing against it, and whether there's safety work outstanding that a dealer will do for free.

All of it takes about twenty minutes and costs almost nothing. Here's the routine.

📌 Key takeaways
  • Check the VIN in at least two physical locations on the car. They must match exactly.
  • Character 10 gives you the model year. If it disagrees with the ad, walk away.
  • Transport Canada's database tells you what's been recalled; the manufacturer's VIN lookup tells you what's outstanding on your car.
  • Run a lien search — Ontario's PPSA registry or Quebec's RDPRM. A lien follows the car, not the seller.

Reading the seventeen characters

Since 1981, vehicles sold in North America carry a standardised 17-character Vehicle Identification Number. First useful fact: the letters I, O and Q never appear in a VIN, precisely because they're confusable with 1 and 0. So if you think you're looking at an O, it's a zero.

The number breaks into blocks:

Characters 1–3: the manufacturer identifier. The first character tells you the region of assembly — a 2 means Canada, 1, 4 or 5 means the United States, 3 is Mexico, J is Japan, K is Korea, W is Germany, S is the United Kingdom. Characters two and three narrow it to the specific manufacturer and division.

Characters 4–8: the vehicle descriptor. Model, body style, engine, restraint system and series. This is where a decoder earns its keep — it lets you confirm that a car advertised as a particular trim with a particular engine actually is one. If the ad says all-wheel drive with the bigger engine and the VIN says otherwise, you've found something worth asking about.

Character 9: the check digit. A calculated value derived from all the other characters. Any legitimate North American VIN will pass the check-digit calculation, and online decoders verify it automatically. A VIN that fails the check digit has either been written down wrong or is not a real VIN — either way, stop and re-verify before going further.

Character 10: the model year. Covered below, because it's the single most useful character for a used buyer.

Character 11: the assembly plant. Rarely important to a buyer, occasionally relevant for parts.

Characters 12–17: the serial number. Sequential production number.

Character 10: the model year

This one is worth committing to memory because it catches misrepresentation instantly.

The tenth character encodes the model year using a cycle of letters and numbers that skips I, O, Q, U, Z and the digit zero. Recent values run: H = 2017, J = 2018, K = 2019, L = 2020, M = 2021, N = 2022, P = 2023, R = 2024, S = 2025, T = 2026.

Why it matters: model year drives value, and a vehicle advertised as one year newer than it is may be priced accordingly. It also affects your financing, since lenders apply vehicle age limits, and your insurance rating. If the tenth character disagrees with the advertisement, that's not a technicality — it's the definitive answer, and it needs explaining before anything else happens.

Note that the model year is not the same as the build date. The label in the driver's door jamb usually shows the actual month and year of manufacture, and a vehicle can be built late in one calendar year as the following model year. That's entirely normal.

Every place the VIN appears

Check at least two, ideally three. Cross-matching is the whole point — a single VIN plate proves nothing on its own.

  • Base of the windshield, driver's side. A small metal plate readable from outside the car. The one everyone knows.
  • Driver's door jamb. A label on the pillar or the door edge. Usually also shows tire pressures, the manufacture date, and the gross vehicle weight rating. This is the label most worth photographing.
  • Engine bay. Often stamped or on a sticker on the firewall or a strut tower.
  • Under the trunk floor or spare tire on some vehicles.
  • The frame or a chassis rail, particularly on trucks.
  • The compliance label. Vehicles built for the Canadian market carry a statement of compliance with the national safety mark. Its presence — or absence — tells you whether the vehicle was originally manufactured for sale in Canada.
  • Paperwork. The registration, the bill of sale, the insurance slip, the vehicle history report, and any safety certificate. Every one of these must show the same seventeen characters.

Photograph each one on your phone. It takes two minutes and gives you a record if anything is disputed later.

Signs of tampering

Rare, but the consequences are severe — a vehicle with an altered VIN can be seized, leaving you with a loan and no car. Things to look at:

  • Any VIN that doesn't match the others. The single biggest red flag, and the reason to check multiple locations.
  • A windshield plate that looks disturbed — scratches around the fasteners, adhesive residue, sitting unevenly, or fasteners that don't match the ones on other cars of the same model.
  • A door jamb label that looks reprinted. Wrong font, wrong finish, colours slightly off, edges that don't sit flat, or a label that looks newer than the car around it.
  • A missing label where there should be one, with an explanation that sounds thin.
  • Stamped numbers that look re-struck — uneven spacing, inconsistent depth, characters at slightly different angles.
  • A seller reluctant to let you look. Asking to photograph the door jamb is a completely normal request. Resistance to it tells you something.
  • A check digit that fails. Run the VIN through a decoder that validates it.

If anything here doesn't add up, don't negotiate around it. Walk away, and if you have real concerns about a stolen vehicle, that's a conversation for the police rather than the seller.

Checking recalls properly

This is where most people do half the job, so it's worth being precise about the two different tools.

Transport Canada's Motor Vehicle Safety Recalls Database. Free and public, searchable by make, model and year. It shows you every recall campaign issued for that vehicle — what the defect was, what the remedy is, and how many units were affected. What it does not do is tell you whether the work was completed on the specific car in front of you.

The manufacturer's VIN lookup. Most brands offer a recall check on their Canadian website where you enter the VIN and get a vehicle-specific answer: outstanding campaigns, and what's already been completed. This is the authoritative source for your car, and it's the one to rely on. A franchised dealer for the brand can also run it through their system.

Then the practical part:

  • Recall work is free, at an authorised dealer for the brand, regardless of the vehicle's age, mileage, or how many owners it's had.
  • An outstanding recall on a used car is common and often innocent. Notices go to the registered owner at the time they're issued, so if the car has changed hands the current owner may genuinely never have been told.
  • Watch for "do not drive" advisories. Some recalls are serious enough that the manufacturer advises against driving the vehicle until repaired. That's a very different situation from a routine software update, and it needs resolving before delivery, not after.
  • Parts availability can be an issue. On some campaigns, dealers are waiting on supply. Ask specifically whether the remedy parts are available, not just whether the dealer will do the work.
  • Get it in writing. Whatever the dealer or seller commits to, put it on the bill of sale with a date. Verbal assurances about recalls have a way of evaporating after delivery.

Lien searches in Ontario and Quebec

The most financially dangerous thing a VIN can reveal, and the check most private buyers skip.

A lien attaches to the vehicle, not to the person. If the seller still owes money on the car and the loan isn't discharged, the lender's security remains registered against that vehicle — and the vehicle can be repossessed from you, even though you paid the seller in full and did nothing wrong.

In Ontario, registered security interests are searchable through the province's personal property security registry. If you're buying privately, Ontario also requires the seller to provide a Used Vehicle Information Package, which includes registration history, previous owners, odometer readings on record, and lien information. Read it rather than filing it — it's genuinely informative, and if a private seller won't produce one, that's a problem.

In Quebec, the equivalent is the RDPRM, the register of personal and movable real rights. Search it by VIN before any money changes hands.

Buying from a franchised or registered dealer largely removes this risk, since they're responsible for delivering clear title — one of the real advantages of a dealer purchase. Private sales put the responsibility on you. More on the trade-offs between private and dealer purchases.

Branding, write-offs and status

Provinces assign a status to vehicles that have been badly damaged, and it follows the VIN.

Ontario brands vehicles as Normal, Rebuilt, Salvage or Irreparable. A Salvage vehicle can't be driven on the road until inspected and rebranded; an Irreparable one can never be registered for road use again. Quebec maintains its own designations for severely damaged vehicles.

Why this matters beyond safety: a branded vehicle is frequently unfinanceable. Many lenders won't advance against a rebuilt vehicle at all, and those that do may impose worse terms. Insurers may also decline to write full comprehensive and collision coverage — which your lender requires. So a branded car can be a dead end for a financed purchase even when the price looks attractive.

Check the branding on the registration document and cross-reference against a vehicle history report. How to read a Canadian vehicle history report.

US imports

Plenty of good vehicles are imported from the United States. They just need extra verification, and the recall angle is specific.

  • Recalls don't map one-to-one across the border. American and Canadian campaigns are issued separately with different numbering, so a vehicle that's clear in the US system may still have applicable Canadian work — or vice versa. Check both the manufacturer's Canadian VIN lookup and the US federal safety database.
  • Confirm the import was done properly. Canada's registrar of imported vehicles process requires vehicles brought in from the US to be made compliant, and part of that process involves obtaining a recall clearance letter from the manufacturer confirming no outstanding recalls at time of import. Ask to see the import documentation.
  • Check for a Canadian compliance label. A vehicle originally built for the US market won't have the Canadian statement of compliance, which is normal for an import but should match the story you're being told.
  • Watch for a US total-loss history. A vehicle written off in a US state and repaired doesn't always carry that history cleanly into a Canadian registration. A history report that covers both countries is worth paying for here.
  • Confirm your lender will finance an import. Some programs restrict them. Ask before you commit.

Getting it onto the paperwork

Verification is only useful if it's tied to the actual transaction. Three things before you sign:

1. The VIN on the bill of sale must match the car. Read it character by character against the door jamb label, not from memory. Transposed digits happen, and a mismatch creates registration and insurance problems later.

2. Open recalls disclosed in writing, with a specific commitment to complete them and a date. "We'll look after it" is not a commitment.

3. Nothing left blank. Every field filled or struck through before your signature goes on the page. The full list of things to ask and bring at signing.

Your twenty-minute VIN routine

  • ✓Photograph the VIN in at least two places — windshield plate and door jamb label at minimum.
  • ✓Confirm every instance matches, including the registration and the bill of sale.
  • ✓Run it through a decoder that validates the check digit and confirms the trim and engine.
  • ✓Check character 10 against the advertised model year. No exceptions on this one.
  • ✓Search Transport Canada's recall database by make, model and year.
  • ✓Run the manufacturer's VIN-specific recall lookup and print or screenshot the result.
  • ✓Ask whether any recall is a "do not drive" and whether remedy parts are available.
  • ✓Run a lien search — Ontario's PPSA registry or Quebec's RDPRM — before any money moves.
  • ✓In an Ontario private sale, insist on the Used Vehicle Information Package and read it.
  • ✓Check the branding status on the registration and confirm your lender and insurer will accept it.
  • ✓For a US import, verify the import paperwork and check recalls in both countries.
  • ✓Get the VIN and the recall commitment written on the bill of sale before signing.

None of this replaces a mechanical inspection — a car can have a perfect VIN history and a failing transmission. But the VIN checks are the cheapest and fastest part of due diligence, and they're the ones that catch the problems no amount of driving the car would reveal. The mechanical side of the checklist.

Frequently asked questions

How do I check a VIN for recalls in Canada?

Use two sources, because they do different jobs. Transport Canada's Motor Vehicle Safety Recalls Database lets you search by make, model and year to see every recall campaign issued for that vehicle — useful for understanding what's been recalled, but not specific to your car. For a definitive answer on whether your particular vehicle has outstanding work, use the manufacturer's own VIN lookup on their Canadian website, which shows whether the repairs were completed on that exact vehicle. Check both, and get the result in writing before you sign.

Where is the VIN located on a car?

There are several locations and you should check at least two or three. The most visible is on a small plate at the base of the windshield on the driver's side, readable from outside the car. There's also a label in the driver's door jamb, usually on the pillar or the door edge, which typically carries tire pressure information and the manufacture date too. Many vehicles have it stamped in the engine bay on the firewall or a strut tower, and some have it on the frame or under the spare tire. Every instance should match exactly.

What does each part of a VIN mean?

A modern VIN is 17 characters and never uses the letters I, O or Q, so anything that looks like one is actually a 1 or a zero. The first three characters identify the manufacturer and where it was built — a 2 in first position means Canada, 1, 4 or 5 means the United States. Characters four through eight describe the vehicle itself: model, body style, engine and restraint system. Character nine is a computed check digit. Character ten is the model year, character eleven is the assembly plant, and the last six are the sequential serial number.

Are recall repairs free in Canada?

Yes. Safety recall work is performed at no charge by an authorised dealer for the brand, regardless of the vehicle's age, how many owners it's had, or whether you're the person who received the notice. That last point matters on a used car — recall notices go to the registered owner at the time, so if the vehicle has changed hands the current owner may never have been told. An outstanding recall on a used car is common and isn't necessarily a sign anyone was hiding anything, but it does need to be completed.

What should I check if the car was imported from the United States?

Check the recall position on both sides of the border, because American and Canadian recall campaigns don't map one-to-one and a Canadian dealer will need the applicable campaign information to do the work. Confirm the vehicle was properly imported and made compliant — the federal registrar of imported vehicles process requires, among other things, a recall clearance letter from the manufacturer, and you want evidence that was obtained. Also confirm the vehicle carries a Canadian statement of compliance, and check whether it was ever declared a total loss in a US state, since branding doesn't always follow a vehicle cleanly across the border.
